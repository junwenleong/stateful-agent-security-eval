We discover that prompt-injection success and tool-execution success are separable safety properties: defences that block injection do not necessarily block execution, and vice versa. We call this the injection-execution dissociation. In LLM agents with persistent memory, malicious instructions are stored at rates exceeding 97.5%, yet downstream execution ranges from 0% to 95% independently of storage rate, so preventing storage alone is insufficient. All tasks are benign proxies that exercise the same channels and tool APIs as harmful deployments.
 A 5,040-run factorial across nine open-source models (N=40 per condition) tests six defences at four architectural layers against delayed-trigger attacks persisting across sessions via RAG. Effectiveness is governed by where a defence sits relative to the attack's authority boundary and by judge capacity: input-layer filters never observe the payload, small retrieval-layer judges cannot separate compliance-framed injection from legitimate policy though frontier-class judges can, and instruction-level hardening is overridden for seven of nine models. Only Memory Sandbox, a tool-layer defence that isolates recalled memory from executable context, reduces attack success to 0% for eight of nine models, though it inverts qwq:32b from 0% to 100% by forcing RAG-fallback reconstruction. A reasoning-mode ablation within Qwen-3-32B reveals a double dissociation, with the bypass replicating across model families: no single schema-layer intervention is safe across both reasoning classes.
 A loaded-corpus frontier evaluation (21 base models, 28 configurations, 3 providers) shows vendor-correlated patterns in our evaluation window: Anthropic blocks predominantly at injection, OpenAI at execution, and a pre-release Gemini endpoint exfiltrates in the majority of runs. Stored-but-dormant payloads thus pose a compositional supply-chain risk in shared memory.
